Raising BER from G to A: Step-by-Step Retrofit Sequence

Independent guidance for Irish homeowners

Key Takeaways

  • Order matters more than budget. Fabric first (insulation, airtightness), then heating system, then renewables. Skip the order and you overpay.
  • G-rated homes qualify for the largest grants. Up to €50,000 through SEAI's deep retrofit supports, more than any other starting BER.
  • A BER assessment is step one, not step five. Get your BER rating survey done before you talk to anyone about heat pumps.
  • Insulation does the heavy lifting. External insulation alone can shift a home two or three sub-bands on its own.
  • Heat pumps need a Heat Pump Test first. Fit one before the fabric is fixed and you'll pay for oversizing you didn't need.
  • Solar PV is the finishing touch, not the starting point. Explore solar PV options once heating demand is already low.
  • A full deep retrofit typically takes 8 to 16 weeks on-site. Plan around that timeline, not the other way around.

If your BER cert has a G on it, you're not alone, and you're not stuck. Nearly one in ten Irish homes still sits in the F or G band, yet homeowners in that exact position can access up to €50,000 in SEAI grant funding to improve BER from G to A. That's not a typo. It's the single biggest grant available anywhere in the retrofit system, precisely because G-rated homes have the furthest to travel.

Most people feel lost when it comes to energy upgrades. They ring an installer, get quoted for a heat pump, and nobody mentions that the walls are leaking heat faster than any system could ever keep up with. That's why we always start with Independent Energy Advice, before a single euro gets spent, so the sequence is right and the money isn't wasted.

Why Improving BER from G to A Takes a Sequence, Not a Shopping List

A G rating usually means an old boiler, thin or missing insulation, and single-glazed windows working against each other all at once. Throwing a heat pump into that mix without fixing the fabric first is one of the most common (and most expensive) mistakes we see.

I take a different approach, the neighbourly one. I explain your home in plain English: where the heat is leaking, whether your radiators can support a modern system, and what steps will actually make a difference.

This is not a sales call and not a quote. It's Independent Energy Advice built around your house, not around a product someone else needs to sell.

Step 1: Get a BER Assessment Before You Improve BER from G to A

You can't fix what you haven't measured. A proper BER rating survey gives you your Leakiness Score, explained in plain English, along with the specific weak points dragging your rating down.

This is where the retrofit roadmap actually starts. Without it, every later decision is a guess.

Step 2: Fix the Fabric First, Insulation Comes Before Anything Else

Walls, attic, floor. In that rough order, fabric upgrades are almost always the cheapest way to buy the biggest BER improvement.

Standard insulation upgrades close the gaps that let heat escape in the first place. For solid-walled or older properties, external insulation is often the single biggest lever available, and we've seen it move homes several sub-bands on its own.

We've watched this play out on real jobs, including an external insulation retrofit in Bohola and another completed in Belmont. Same starting point, low BER, same first move: fix the walls before touching the heating system.

Step 3: The Heat Pump Test, Don't Buy a System Before This

No engineering jargon here. Just one question: can your home actually hold heat well enough for a heat pump to work efficiently?

If the fabric isn't fixed, a heat pump gets oversized to compensate, and oversized systems cost more to buy and more to run. This is the Heat Pump Test we run before recommending anyone spend on air source heat pumps.

Replacing an oil boiler with an air-to-water heat pump can move a home 3 to 5 sub-bands on the BER scale, but only once the building is ready to receive it. Skip the fabric work and that number drops fast.

Step 4: Add Solar PV Once the Fabric Is Sorted

Solar PV is a good add-on. It is not a fix for a leaky house.

A standard 3.5 kWp solar PV system typically adds 1 to 2 sub-bands to your BER. That's meaningful, but it's the icing, not the cake. Get the fabric and heating right first, then let solar do the rest of the work.

Step 5: Use the Deep Retrofit Programme to Improve BER from G to A Affordably

This is where the money conversation gets serious. SEAI's deep retrofit programme exists specifically for homes starting at F or G, and the grant scales with the severity of your starting point.

A 2026 government budget of €558m for retrofits, and 58,000+ homes already upgraded through SEAI programmes, shows this isn't a niche scheme. It's the mainstream path homeowners are actually using to improve BER from G to A.

Combine that with the Better Energy Homes Scheme for smaller individual measures, and the grant-stage sequencing gets more complex, not less. Getting the order of applications wrong can mean missing funding altogether. This is exactly where Independent Energy Advice earns its keep, because grant eligibility rules shift year to year and nobody selling you a system has a reason to walk you through them properly.

Official 15-Band Irish BER Scale & NZEB Benchmarks (Part L 2026 / EPBD)

The Irish Building Energy Rating (BER) framework uses a precise 15-band primary energy scale from G up to A1/A0. Transforming a home from a G rating up to A2 achieves compliance with Ireland's Nearly Zero Energy Building (NZEB) standard.

📐 Official Irish Part L / NZEB Compliance Criteria (A2 Standard)

  • Energy Performance Coefficient (EPC): ≤ 0.30 (70% reduction in primary energy compared to 2005 baseline)
  • Carbon Performance Coefficient (CPC): ≤ 0.35 (65% reduction in regulated CO₂ emissions)
  • Renewable Energy Ratio (RER): ≥ 20% (minimum renewable primary energy contribution)
  • Heat Loss Indicator (HLI): ≤ 2.0 W/K/m² (mandatory threshold for heat pump grant eligibility under NSAI SR50-2)

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to improve BER from G to A in Ireland?

Most 3-bed semi-detached homes see a net cost of €22,000 to €38,000 after SEAI grants, though G-rated homes can access up to €50,000 in support through the deep retrofit programme. The final figure depends heavily on wall type, attic condition, and whether external insulation is needed.

Is it worth improving BER from G to A in 2026?

Yes, with the 2026 government retrofit budget sitting at €558m and heat pump grants at €12,500, the financial support is stronger than it's ever been. Combined with 60-80% reductions in heating bills after a deep retrofit, the payback case is solid.

What's the correct order to improve BER from G to A?

Start with a BER assessment, move to insulation and airtightness, then upgrade the heating system, and finish with solar PV. Doing it in reverse order almost always leads to an oversized, overpriced heating system.

How long does a full G to A retrofit take?

A full deep retrofit typically takes 8 to 16 weeks on-site once work begins, though the planning and grant-approval stages beforehand can add several more weeks. Homeowners who get Independent Energy Advice early tend to move through the grant stages faster.

Do I need a heat pump to reach an A rating from a G rating?

In almost every case, yes, since a heat pump can move a home 3 to 5 sub-bands on its own. But it should only go in after a Heat Pump Test confirms the fabric can support it efficiently.

Does solar PV alone improve BER from G to A?

No. A 3.5 kWp solar PV system typically adds just 1 to 2 sub-bands, which isn't enough on its own to bridge the gap from G to A. It works best as the final step after fabric and heating upgrades are already in place.

Why should I get Independent Energy Advice before hiring an installer?

Installers are naturally inclined to recommend what they sell, which isn't always the right first step for your specific home. Independent Energy Advice looks at your BER assessment, your Leakiness Score, and your budget first, so the sequence of work actually matches what your house needs.
